* 1 Introduction

Restricted data availability or model dimensionality make it that some
Computable General Equilibrium (CGE) models have to be written as
single country models.  More often than not, the small open economy
(SOE) assumption is chosen for formulating single country models which
assume global market prices to be fixed. Without careful calibration,
fix global market prices can make imported or exported quantities very
sensitive to domestic price changes. The classical approach to such
calibration was given by de Melo and Robinson (1989). It works around
the issue of fix global market prices by calibrating finite price
elasticities of both imports and exports in the SOE. This is achieved
by making the provision of domestically produced goods on local and
international markets subject to imperfect transformability (described
by a finite elasticity of transformation EOT) and intermediate inputs
subject to imperfect substitutability between imports and domestically
supplied varieties (given by a finite EOS). While this yields
reasonable quantity responses to price shocks, Lanz and Rutherford
(2016) and Schreiber et al. (2024) (as well as sources cited therein)
show that thusly calibrated SOE models cannot keep track of welfare
impacts in a way consistent with global models due to missing
terms-of-trade effects.

A popular fix is to switch to a large open economy (LOE)
model. Developed by James Markusen\footnote{%
https://www.colorado.edu/faculty/markusen/computation-manuscript },
this framework has found application in models such as the MIT's USREP
model (Yuan et al., 2019). But the correct calibration of the price
elasticities of the world's export demand and import supply is not
self evident and the only guidance for this I could find is a recent
paper by Schreiber et al. (2024). They use the GTAPinGAMS model for
in-model estimation of the elasticities and calibrate a LOE version of
the US economy. They show their calibrated LOE model to be very
successful in reproducing the results of the global GTAPinGAMS model.

In this paper, I go a similar yet easier route for calibrating an
improved SOE model. I observe that the missing terms-of-trade effects
in SOE models is a direct consequence of the assumption that both
import and export prices are fixed at global market prices (the small
modelled economy cannot affect these). But this is not the situation
faced by small countries in a global CGE model such as the GTAP model
(Corong et al. 2017): The Armington assumption makes it that country
A's output of commodity $i$ is traded-off with imports from elsewhere
by another country B's importers at a (constant) elasticity of
substitution (EOS) of, say, $\sigma^{m}_i$. In the limit of the ratio
of the size of A's economy over the size of the global economy going
to 0, the activity levels and overall import demands of other
countries' sectors will be indeed unaffected by price levels in the
SOE. But the market power of the SOE's exports /within/ other
countries' overall imports is unaffected by SOE being small compared
to the world. Caliendo and Feenstra (2024) kindly showed that in the
limit of the SOE being of size zero compared to the global economy,
the global demand for its exports has a price elasticity that
corresponds to $\sigma^{m}_i$ in above notation.

I therefore propose an improved SOE (how about ``small armington trade
economy'' or SATE for short?) model, where global demand for the
SATE's export of commodity $i$ has a price elasticity informed by the
sector's Armington elasticity $\sigma^{m}_i$ and import prices are
fixed at exogenous global price levels.

In this paper I show how well the SATE model compares to a global
version of GTAPinGAMS. It becomes evident that Schreiber et al.'s
careful calibration of the LOE model is superior to my approach but
that the deviations of SATE's results is does not exceed the
sensitivity of GTAPinGAMS results with respect to uncertain Armington
elasticities. The straight forward calibration of the SATE model makes
it an attractive choice for single-country CGE models.

* 3 Comparison between SATE and the global GTAPinGAMS model
** Scenario design
model verions:
gtap vs gtapingams: gtap views domestic output as a homogenous good
that can be both sold domestically and exported. GTAPinGAMS assumes an
finite EOT between exported and domestically marketed version of the
output. It compensates the introduction of the finite EOT by
increasing the substitutability between imports and domestic goods. In
line with Lanz and Rutherford, I assume the same EOT and armington EOS
for the global and the SOE version of the model.

trade closures:
- GMR: standard global CGE model
- GMR_LS: the global CGE model with EOS between imports from different
  source countries halved.  Ahmad et al. (2021) show that elasticities
  of substitution in Armington trade aggregators are quite uncertain
  and that GTAP estimates are at the upper end of the range of
  estimates. This motivates GMR_LS as a sensitivity analysis.
- SOE: SOE model with fix world prices. This is doomed to produce
  bang-bang solutions win the gtap model verion
- SATE: (called sop in the model code) This model assumes market power
  of SATE sectors in their export markets. Price elasticities of
  world's export demand is given by the respective Armington
  elasticity.
